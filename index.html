  • Code jobagent — a job search that keeps its own books 2026 · Python Automates the parts of a job search that are the same every time: tailoring the resume to the posting, filling in the portal application, keeping track of where everything stands straight out of Gmail, and drafting outreach in my own writing voice. It never sends anything. How it works, four decisions worth defending, and the repo. PythonGmail APIscikit-learnagentsMCP →

    I built jobagent for my own job search and ran it on that search the whole way through. That is where most of the design came from — each piece exists because something broke without it.

    It never sends anything. Every email it writes is an unsent Gmail draft that I read and send myself. After each run, an agent reads the whole search and leaves a brief of what needs me first.

    How it works

    1. Tailor. It prepares the resume against the posting rather than sending the same file everywhere — the wording that matches what the role actually asks for, drawn from my own material.
    2. Apply. It fills in the portal application, so the same name, dates and standing answers aren't retyped into a different form for the hundredth time. I review a completed application instead of writing one.
    3. Read. Every few hours it reads new mail. Application confirmations become sheet rows; rejections, interview invites and human replies update the status. Each hiring system formats its email differently, so a parser handles the common ones and two scikit-learn classifiers catch the wording the rules miss.
    4. Research. For an application with no contact yet, it works out who the right person to write to is, and how far that can be trusted, before anything gets drafted. What it finds stays on my machine — none of it is in the repository, and none of it is on this page.
    5. Draft. The email is written from a retrieval index over my own past outreach, so it sounds like me, then checked against how I actually write before it becomes a draft.
    6. Judge. An agent whose only tools are the project's own functions picks which cold applications deserve research today, drafts due follow-ups, and writes the brief. It has no tool that sends mail, and its budget is a hard cap in code.

    Four decisions worth defending

    Learning behaviour instead of assuming it. The follow-up scheduler needed a wait interval. Rather than pick a round number, it measures: pair each follow-up with the message before it to the same person and take the median gap. It came out shorter than I'd have guessed, which is the point — the interval comes from how I actually follow up, not from a figure that sounded about right. Getting the training labels right mattered more than the model. The obvious label — "is this a later message in the thread?" — is mostly replies to people who had already written back, and training on those produces a follow-up that opens "Thanks for getting back to me!" addressed to someone who never did. The labels come from Gmail thread state instead: a send with no inbound message before it is a genuine nudge into silence. That distinction is the whole feature.

    Deriving state instead of storing it. Every follow-up rule reads off the live Gmail thread rather than a local state file — they replied, skip; two sends of mine already, skip; a pending draft, skip. Nothing can drift out of sync with what was actually sent, and re-runs are safe by construction.

    Refusing to guess. When no contact can be verified, the run writes a .SKIPPED file explaining why instead of inventing a plausible name. A fabricated detail in a cold email is worse than no email: the recipient notices.

    Public code, private search. The repository is public; the search it ran is not in it. Identity, resume, application answers and anything it ever learned about another person live in gitignored files with .example templates beside them, and the test fixtures use invented people. Nothing about the sender is hard-coded, so anyone can clone it and run their own search without inheriting mine.

    What it deliberately doesn't do

    • No LinkedIn scraping. Their terms prohibit automated collection, and breaking that risks your account. The helper only builds a search URL — you browse and pick the contact yourself.
    • No auto-sending. Every email is created as a Gmail draft. You click Send yourself, every time.

    Built with

    Python 3.12 · Gmail, Sheets and Calendar APIs · scikit-learn · Claude (API, CLI, or an MCP server the project exposes) or Meta's Muse for the research and writing steps. Everything except research and drafting runs with no model at all.

    The architecture is enforced rather than described: one module declares which layer each file belongs to, and a test fails if any import points upward.

  • Capital markets CPO analysis Sep 2026 · Optical networking · 18 min Optical networking has re-rated almost as hard as memory, on a fraction of the coverage — and on backlogs that haven't turned into revenue yet. What the supply chain actually looks like, why networking is the bottleneck in AI infrastructure, how copper gives way to co-packaged optics across scale-up and scale-out, and the cost, capacity, materials and yield problems keeping CPO out of mass production.     We've seen memory stocks rise at almost astronomical rates. Everyone knows SK Hynix, Micron, SanDisk, CXMT, Western Digital, and others. However, there is another group within semiconductors that has also experienced a meteoric rise in stock prices but has received significantly less media and analyst coverage: optical networking companies. These include AAOI, AXT, Coherent, Lumentum, Landmark Optoelectronics, and Zhongji Innolight, to name a few.

    These companies sit across the photonics and optical communications supply chain, which is considerably more complicated, less defined and consolidated, and uncertain than memory. It stretches from upstream materials and components — substrates, wafers, raw materials, FAUs, lasers — all the way downstream to transceivers and optical modules. Unlike memory, where the prevailing technologies and product roadmaps are relatively well defined, photonics is still evolving rapidly, with multiple competing technologies and architectures.

    At the same time, the industry is facing significant constraints in upstream materials and in manufacturing cost, yield, and capacity. Many companies already have significant order backlogs, but the revenue has not materialised yet. Forward multiples are based on projected demand in 2027, 2028, or even 2029, rather than on revenue being recognised today, which makes them particularly vulnerable to the current macro environment. We are seeing more and more news and confirmed reports of delays in adoption. What follows is the adoption of optical technologies across both scale-up and scale-out architectures, and the various forms those technologies can take.

    Why networking is the bottleneck

    Photonics sits within a very crucial part of the AI infrastructure supply chain: networking. GPUs and CPUs are only useful if they can communicate with each other fast enough. With thousands of accelerators operating inside a data centre, the communication requirements become enormous. NVIDIA's GB300 can place 72 GPUs and 36 CPUs in a single rack, creating an extremely high-bandwidth communication requirement.

    It helps to split networking into two layers. Scale-up is communication between GPUs inside one tightly coupled domain, today usually a single rack, and it still relies mainly on high-speed copper. Scale-out is communication between racks, and it increasingly relies on optics. The rest of this piece follows that split.

    In NVIDIA's architecture, this can involve 1.6T optical transceiver modules — an aggregate 1.6 terabits per second, built from 8 × 200G lanes — with a total of 216 optical modules required for the system. We expect networking technology to keep advancing on both sides. On the scale-up side, from copper toward a mixture of copper and CPO; on the scale-out side, from traditional optical transceivers toward CPO. Bandwidth requirements keep climbing with it. 800G and 1.6T are the mainstream standards today. By 2028, 3.2T is expected to enter mass production, while 6.4T could reach the sampling stage. Looking ahead to Feynman, the generation after Vera Rubin, the architecture is not yet entirely clear, so I won't go far into it below. But it is evident that CPO becomes a standard and the most important component of networking for both scale-up and scale-out.

    Scale-up: NVIDIA

    Using NVIDIA as an industry benchmark, the GB300, which uses the Oberon rack system, relies on copper for its scale-up connections. Vera Rubin, also on Oberon, is likewise expected to use copper. Rubin Ultra, expected to use the Kyber and Oberon rack systems respectively, is when traditional copper wiring begins to be replaced. The architecture could involve PCB midplanes and copper, or CPO and copper. The choice of routing architecture will depend in part on the switches used within the system.

    A small note here. There is still some uncertainty, but this appears to be the most concise way to think about it. In 2028, NVIDIA is expected to introduce two versions of Rubin Ultra: NVL144 and NVL576. The first is expected to use PCB midplane technology with flyover copper wire in the switch tray; the second a combination of copper and optics. NVL576 combines eight Oberon racks into a single system, which may blur the traditional definition of scale-up somewhat. I would still consider any architecture where the GPUs are connected within the same system to be part of scale-up networking.

    We know there are limits to traditional copper. Copper cables become increasingly thick and heavy as bandwidth requirements rise, while signal integrity deteriorates beyond roughly 2 to 3 metres, and sometimes shorter. That applies primarily to passive copper cables. There is an upgrade: Active Electrical Cables (AECs) incorporate retimers and other active components to extend usable transmission distances and maintain signal integrity. But AECs remain subject to the fundamental physical limitations of copper, particularly as bandwidth and link distances continue to increase.

    This brings us to another potential solution: the PCB midplane. A midplane has lower bandwidth than a direct copper cable for certain applications, but it offers several important advantages for high-density AI systems. Rather than relying on large bundles of individual copper cables, a midplane provides short, direct board-level electrical connections and can integrate multiple routing layers into a highly concentrated structure. That enables a High-Density Interconnect (HDI) architecture, improving space efficiency while helping maintain signal integrity over short distances.

    The NVL144 combines a massive multilayer PCB midplane, or orthogonal backplane, with copper flyover cables. The midplane serves as the central structural and electrical routing layer, enabling a high-density, 90-degree rack layout while eliminating the need for thousands of individual internal cables. It handles the dense, short-reach board-level connections, while flyover cables provide flexibility for connections that need greater reach or a different path.

    Some major players in PCB manufacturing for AI and HPC include Victory Giant Technology, WUS Printed Circuit, Tripod Technology, and Gold Circuit Electronics. TTM Technologies, while currently smaller in this particular market, is the major U.S. PCB player and has high potential. Victory Giant is one of the leading players in AI and high-performance computing PCBs and is pursuing aggressive capacity expansion. For one of the most critical materials in the PCB stack, copper-clad laminate (CCL), companies such as Elite Material (EMC) and Doosan are competing in the high-end, low-loss segment with M9-class materials, which are widely expected in Rubin Ultra. Elite Material appears particularly well positioned; based on current industry developments and its certification activity with NVIDIA, it could emerge as a major, or potentially dominant, player in high-end AI CCL.

    The second version is NVL576, which connects eight Oberon racks, each containing 72 Rubin Ultra GPUs, using CPO between the racks in a two-tier, all-to-all network, while the scale-up connections within each rack remain copper-based. The longer-term solution will likely shift toward heavier use of optical interconnects, particularly in the Kyber rack system and even more so in Feynman.

    Optics have a fundamental advantage over electrical copper links: optical signals can travel significantly farther without the same degree of degradation, while optical links provide much higher bandwidth density and are less susceptible to electromagnetic interference. However, optics are not automatically the answer today. The technology still has to clear manufacturing yield, cost, power consumption, packaging, thermal management, and production capacity. More on all of that below.

    It's worth clarifying that this gradual shift from copper toward optical interconnects applies only to AI servers and high-performance computing infrastructure. Traditional servers are still largely dependent on copper, for two reasons. First, optical interconnects are significantly more expensive than conventional copper, and for traditional workloads the additional cost is often difficult to justify. Second, traditional servers generally do not have the same bandwidth, latency, and scale-up requirements. And within AI servers, copper is unlikely to be completely replaced — usage should decline as optics spread, but both PCB midplanes and CPO-based scale-up architectures retain some copper content.

    Scale-up: Google

    Any discussion of optical scale-up has to include Google's TPU. Google has used optics in its scale-up network since TPU v4, where optical circuit switches (OCS) connect groups of 64 chips into pods containing thousands of chips. The same approach continues into future generations.

    To understand why Google adopted optics so early, it helps to compare the two architectures. NVIDIA groups GPUs into a single domain connected through NVSwitch, which acts as a central switching layer. If GPU 15 wants to communicate with GPU 38, it sends the data to an NVSwitch, which forwards it to the destination — exactly one switch hop. Google, by contrast, does not use a central switch inside the pod. Each TPU connects directly to its neighbours through Inter-Chip Interconnect (ICI) ports, arranged in a 3D torus. Each chip has six neighbours: left, right, front, back, up, and down. To reach a TPU that is not a direct neighbour, data hops from chip to chip through the torus. Routing is therefore distributed across the network, which requires significant software engineering to coordinate.

    This topology gave Google a stronger reason to adopt optics early, for three reasons. First, physical distance: a torus has wraparound links that connect the edges of the grid back to each other, and in a large pod some of those links span racks, pushing beyond the practical reach of copper. Second, flexibility: optical circuit switches let Google reconfigure the topology in software, partition a pod into slices for different workloads, and route around failed chips. Third, latency: every additional hop adds latency, so shorter and more direct optical paths become increasingly valuable as pods scale.

    Scale-out: NVIDIA

    Using the Oberon system and NVL72 GB300 as a reference, the system can involve 1.6T optical transceiver modules, 8 × 200G lanes, 216 optical modules in total. In the Vera Rubin Oberon architecture there are two configurations. One still uses 1.6T optical transceivers, with a 1.6T CPO ToR switch and 8 × 200G lanes, but with a higher total number of connections — roughly double the modules of the GB300 generation, 432 for the system. The other adopts a CPO solution, combining 1.6T optical transceivers with optical switching. Looking ahead to Rubin Ultra and the Kyber rack system, both configurations are expected to move to 3.2T optical transceivers paired with 3.2T optical switches.

    Evidently, unlike scale-up networking, which still relies heavily on copper, pluggable optical transceivers are the mainstream solution for scale-out today, given the longer distances and higher bandwidth involved. But as bandwidth continues to increase, the industry appears to be gradually moving toward CPO. I use CPO here as a general reference for that transition. Depending on the specific architecture, the eventual solution may be closer to NPO than to true CPO. Regardless of whether the industry ultimately adopts CPO, NPO, or a combination of both, the broader trend is toward moving optical components closer to the switching or compute silicon and away from traditional pluggable transceivers.

    From pluggables to co-packaged optics

    This is a useful way to classify the roadmap. Today the industry is still largely centred on pluggable optics, where the transceiver sits at the front panel and connects to the ASIC through relatively long electrical traces. The next step moves the optics closer to the ASIC: On-Board Optics (OBO), where the optical engine is placed directly on the PCB, then Near-Package Optics (NPO), where the engine is positioned much closer to the ASIC on a high-performance substrate. The next major step is Co-Packaged Optics (CPO), where the optical engine and ASIC are integrated within the same package, substantially shortening the electrical path between them. The last is the Optoelectronic Integrated Circuit (OEIC), where the optical function is integrated into the chip itself — far too advanced for current technology.

    CPO is a sharp break from today's pluggables. A pluggable is a standalone, hot-swappable module in the switch's front panel; CPO places the optics directly on the same package as the switch chip or GPU. Pluggables are dominated by two kinds. EML modules pair each lane with its own electro-absorption modulated laser built on an indium phosphide (InP) chip, with the laser inside the module. They deliver excellent signal quality at high speed but are costly and supply-constrained. Silicon photonics (SiPh) modules build the optics on silicon wafers and share one continuous-wave (CW) laser across several lanes. Because many optical functions sit on one silicon chip, they use fewer discrete parts, pack more bandwidth into less space, and cost less at scale — and those advantages grow as speeds move from 800G to 1.6T and beyond.

    Silicon photonics is also the foundation of CPO. The role of EMLs is expected to gradually diminish, while CW lasers gain share, potentially approaching 50% by 2028, and become the mainstream solution in the CPO era. Both rely on a DSP to clean up the signal, which becomes a major power and cost burden at 800G and 1.6T. Still, pluggables are easy to replace when they fail and have better yield, since each module is tested on its own. This is the part that gets replaced and goes obsolete with the mass adoption of CPO.

    Inside a CPO switch

    This is roughly what a CPO switch architecture looks like at a high level. It essentially replaces the DSP.

    At the bottom is the substrate or interposer, which provides the physical and electrical connections between the different components. At the centre is the switch ASIC, the core of the system, directing data traffic between the optical links. Surrounding it are the optical engines. Each engine consists primarily of two parts: a Photonic Integrated Circuit (PIC) and an Electronic Integrated Circuit (EIC). The PIC contains the key optical components — modulators, photodetectors, wavelength multiplexers (MUX) and demultiplexers (DEMUX). The EIC contains the key electronic components — drivers and transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs). The engines connect to the external optical network through fiber attach units (FAUs).

    The laser or light source is a critical component of CPO, with different technologies trading off reach, power efficiency, bandwidth density, cost, and maturity. It needs emphasising that this is still an external light source. CW lasers, particularly high-power InP lasers used as external light sources for silicon-photonics engines, are the leading approach and can support both scale-up and scale-out. VCSELs are another option, particularly for shorter-reach scale-up links; they benefit from a mature ecosystem, high-speed modulation, strong energy efficiency, and potentially lower cost, although their reach is generally shorter. MicroLEDs are an emerging option for very short-reach interconnects, with potential advantages in energy efficiency, latency, and cost, but they remain less mature and cover less distance.

    The signal flow can then be understood step by step. On the transmit side, the switch ASIC sends an electrical signal to the EIC, where the driver drives the modulator on the PIC. In the external-laser SiPh implementation, the laser provides the continuous-wave light, and the modulator converts the electrical data into an optical signal. The optical signal then passes through the FAU and into the fiber. On the receive side, the optical signal enters through the FAU and reaches the PIC, where the photodetector converts it back into an electrical signal. That signal goes to the TIA, which amplifies and conditions it before sending it back toward the switch ASIC.

    Who actually builds it

    At the system level, Broadcom and NVIDIA lead CPO development, with Marvell and MediaTek also building solutions. TSMC is the default foundry for the photonic chips, though reports suggest UMC and Intel may win a piece of the business. Beneath these chip designers, vertical integration runs through the supply chain, and several players stand out.

    Coherent and Lumentum are the two most important laser suppliers, but they are generally seen as strong in different ways. Coherent leads on output: it has the broadest product range, from lasers and EMLs to silicon photonics and finished transceivers, and it was the first to move indium phosphide production onto larger 6-inch wafers. Lumentum is widely viewed as the technology leader at the cutting edge — early to ship 200G-per-lane EMLs for 1.6T modules, and a leading supplier of the ultra-high-power CW lasers that CPO requires. NVIDIA's $2 billion investment in each suggests how important they are.

    Marvell supplies the DSPs inside most high-speed transceivers, builds silicon photonics engines, and strengthened its optical interconnect and CPO position by acquiring Celestial AI. TSMC manufactures the photonic chips and provides the COUPE advanced packaging that ties optics to the switch — an irreplaceable role in manufacturing. InnoLight, the world's largest transceiver maker, is moving upstream into silicon photonics to get a slice of the CPO era. In Japan, Sumitomo Electric makes indium phosphide lasers and EMLs as well as optical fiber and connectivity products, giving it a presence across both the light source and the cabling CPO systems depend on.

    Evidently, many of these companies play multiple roles across the value chain, producing everything from lasers and photonic chips to finished optical modules, and in some cases competing directly with their own customers. That highlights the uncertainty surrounding how the technology will evolve and how market share may ultimately be distributed. Beyond the major players there is also a long tail of smaller specialists — component manufacturers, packaging and assembly providers — filling critical gaps with specialised components, manufacturing capacity, and technical expertise.

    Why CPO isn't here yet

    Talk about how much more distance it can cover, how much better the signal is, how much more advanced the technology is. There is so much to praise — so why is CPO not yet in mass production?

    Cost. The exact price is uncertain and difficult to determine, and much of it is confidential. But it is significantly more expensive than optical transceivers, and far more so than AECs and copper. The replacement cost is much higher too. When a pluggable transceiver breaks down, you replace the transceiver. With CPO, the optical engine is integrated much more closely with the switch package, so a failure requires replacing a much larger portion of the system rather than one module. This is also one of the reasons the laser source is kept external. Imagine the laser integrated into the package and then failing: given that lasers have a significantly shorter service life than other components, how much more expensive and complicated would it be to replace the whole assembly? The other concern is overheating taking everything down with it, which becomes a bigger issue with the laser inside the package.

    Capacity. Foundry capacity and laser supply are the two primary constraints. Take COUPE from TSMC, an enabling technology for CPO. According to TrendForce, TSMC's PIC capacity is expected to increase from around 500 wafers per month to 10,000 by the second quarter of 2026, rise further to 15,000 by the fourth quarter, and reach at least 25,000 per month by 2028.

    TSMC's strength is not only foundry capacity but also advanced packaging, which is particularly important for integrating photonics with compute and makes TSMC the number one player here as well. But advanced foundry and packaging capacity is already constrained, so this will likely still be insufficient to meet demand. Intel's Optical Compute Interconnect (OCI) is another interesting technology that could let Intel compete in this market, but it is still in testing and development, so its ability to compete at scale remains to be demonstrated.

    Materials. The laser source is significant because of the underlying semiconductor materials and the complexity of manufacturing them. In addition to silicon, these materials are used because of their ability to efficiently emit and manipulate light. Gallium arsenide (GaAs) and indium phosphide (InP) are two of the key ones, with InP particularly scarce and increasingly important for advanced optical applications. Growing InP crystals is difficult because phosphorus is highly volatile and can evaporate easily during crystal growth, making the material harder to manufacture at scale. Sumitomo Electric Industries, AXT Inc. (United States, with manufacturing in China), and JX Advanced Metals are the three major players in the InP substrate supply chain. On top of that, China put InP under export licensing in February 2025, requiring a permit for every customer order, further constraining an already inadequate supply. The same tightness applies to EML and CW lasers: demand is driven by the rapid expansion of AI servers, migration to higher speeds, and the rising number of optical connections per data centre. Coherent and Lumentum are actively expanding capacity, but supply may still remain tight.

    Yield. Exact figures are confidential, but CPO faces significantly greater yield challenges than conventional pluggable transceivers and copper. That is largely a result of the complexity of advanced packaging. Silicon and InP have different coefficients of thermal expansion, meaning they expand and contract at different rates as temperatures change, so one side stretches more than the other; repeated thousands of times, it leads to malfunction. CPO also requires the integration of multiple components and processes, including high-precision alignment and bonding at very small dimensions. These processes need highly specialised equipment and expertise, and even small alignment or bonding errors can affect optical performance. The packaging issue extends to FAUs as well, making the whole thing very hard to produce. Finally, because CPO is still relatively new and less mature, testing and screening are less established than for conventional pluggables, which makes it harder to identify and correct defects early — another challenge for achieving consistently high yields.

    Where that leaves it

    Put the pieces together and the direction is not really in dispute. Networking is the binding constraint on AI infrastructure, copper runs out of road somewhere around two to three metres, and every roadmap on the table — NVIDIA's, Google's, Broadcom's — walks the optics closer to the silicon. CPO is where that ends up. What is in dispute is when, in what form (CPO, NPO, or some mix of the two), and who keeps the margin once it arrives.

    That gap between direction and timing is the whole problem with the stocks. The technology is years from settled while the multiples already discount 2027 to 2029 demand. Cost, foundry and packaging capacity, InP supply, and yield are each independently capable of pushing adoption out by a year, and most of them are physical constraints that money cannot solve quickly — you cannot tell a phosphorus crystal to grow faster, and TSMC's advanced packaging line is oversubscribed before CPO gets near it. Meanwhile the backlogs are real but unconverted, and the companies holding them compete with their own customers across half the layers of the stack.

    So what I would watch is not demand. Demand is not the question. It is conversion: CPO shipping in volume rather than sampling, COUPE capacity landing on something like the schedule it has been given, InP substrate supply loosening or tightening further, and the first credible yield numbers out of anyone's packaging line. Those are what turn a backlog into revenue. Until they move, these multiples are a bet on a timeline more than on a technology — and timelines in photonics have a long history of slipping to the right.
